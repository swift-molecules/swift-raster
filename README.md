# swift-raster

Use this package when you need a grid of pixels as a value: a width, a height and one pixel per position, over a pixel type you choose. Start from `Raster<Pixel>`. It stores, crops, samples, resamples, warps and convolves, and it traverses with `map`, `zip` and `reduce`. It also turns shapes into pixels: `fragments(of:width:height:empty:_:)` finds the nearest triangle at each pixel centre, and with the `Geometry` trait `coverage(of:rule:width:height:tolerance:)` gives the part of each pixel that a `Geometry.Path` of swift-geometry covers. It ships no pixel type and knows no colour, channel order, transfer function or blend rule: what a pixel means is stated by the type you put in, so an 8-bit display-encoded pixel and a floating-point linear-light pixel are two different instances and cannot be mixed up. Every operation that has to combine pixels takes the combining function from you. To blend one picture over another, pass the blend to `zip`; the arithmetic for that lives in the package that owns the compositing standard, not here. Coverage is a raster of numbers and a fragment is handed to your function: what either does to a picture is yours to say.

## Laws

- A raster holds exactly `width × height` pixels, stored row by row: the pixel at (x, y) is `pixels[y × width + x]`. Construction from a pixel list fails with `.count` when the list has another length or the product does not fit a signed word. Rows are packed; there is no stride and no padding, so equality and hashing compare the size and the pixels and nothing else.
- A raster with no columns or no rows is valid and empty. A raster of 5 × 0 is not equal to one of 0 × 5.
- `init(width:height:_:)` asks for the pixels in row order, (0, 0), (1, 0), …, and stops at the first failure. `subscript(x:y:)` and `row(_:)` trap outside the raster.
- `cropped(to:)` is the region's pixels as a raster of the region's size, and fails with `.bounds` unless the region lies inside the raster. An empty region on the far corner lies inside. `replacing(x:y:with:)` puts another raster at a position, changes no other pixel, and fails with `.bounds` unless it fits. Cropping what was just replaced gives the other raster back.
- `map` visits every pixel in row order, keeps the size, may change the pixel type, and stops at the first failure. `zip(_:_:)` combines two rasters of the same size pixel by pixel in row order, and is `nil` when the sizes differ. `reduce` folds the pixels in row order. The function's typed error is the operation's typed error.
- `mean(against:_:)` is the sum of the error you give for each pair of pixels, divided by the number of pixels; it is `nil` for rasters of different sizes and for rasters with no pixels. `psnr(against:peak:squared:)` is ten times the base-ten logarithm of the peak squared over that mean, in decibels, where your function gives a pixel pair's squared error averaged over its channels. No error at all gives +∞, also for two empty rasters, and different sizes give `nil`.
- Pixel (x, y) covers the square from (x, y) up to but not including (x + 1, y + 1), and its centre is (x + ½, y + ½). `sample(at:_:edge:mixing:)` with `.nearest` gives the pixel whose square holds the point. With `.bilinear` it interpolates between the four pixels whose centres surround the point, first along x in each of the two rows, then along y between the results. At a pixel centre both give that pixel, exactly and without calling the interpolation; along a row or a column of centres only one interpolation is made.
- The interpolation you pass is `(from, to, parameter) -> Pixel`, with the parameter in 0…1: the pixel that is `parameter` of the way from the first to the second. It is the only thing the filtering operations ever ask of a pixel. For nearest sampling it is never called.
- `Raster.Edge` says which pixel stands at a position outside the raster. `.extend` is the nearest pixel inside. `.wrap` repeats the raster, so position p is p modulo the width. `.mirror` reflects it about each border with the border pixel repeated, so the positions beyond `a b c` read `c b a a b c c b a`. `.constant(pixel)` is that pixel everywhere outside. Rows follow the same rule as columns.
- Sampling an empty raster traps, except with `.constant`, which answers the constant. A sample position must be finite; beyond ±2⁶² it is clamped.
- `warped(width:height:_:edge:mixing:from:)` makes a raster of the given size whose pixel at each destination centre is the sample at the source point the function returns for it. The map therefore runs from destination to source, which is the direction a resampler needs. `warped(by:width:height:_:edge:mixing:)` is the same for an affine map given as `Matrix<2, 3, Scalar>`, applied to (x, y, 1). The raster never inverts a matrix: to move a picture by a transform, pass that transform's inverse. The identity map returns the raster under both samplings, a translation by whole pixels moves pixels exactly, and a map onto a window of the raster equals `cropped(to:)`.
- `resampled(width:height:_:mixing:)` changes the size, first along the rows and then along the columns. Each new pixel is an average of old ones with non-negative weights that are normalised over the pixels that exist, so nothing outside the raster is ever read and no edge is needed. `.nearest` takes the old pixel under the new pixel's centre. `.box` weighs each old pixel by how much of the new pixel's footprint it covers: shrinking by a whole factor gives each block's mean, enlarging by a whole factor repeats pixels. `.triangle` weighs by distance from the new centre, reaching one old pixel when enlarging and one new pixel when shrinking: enlarging equals bilinear sampling with `.extend`. Resampling to the same size returns the raster under every filter, and resampling to no columns or no rows gives an empty raster. Resampling an empty raster to a size that has pixels traps.
- A `Raster.Kernel` is an odd number of weights, none negative, with the middle one at the pixel itself. Construction divides them by their sum, and fails with `.empty`, `.even`, `.weight` (a negative or non-finite weight) or `.zero` (nothing to normalise). `Raster.Kernel.box(radius:)` has `2 × radius + 1` equal weights. `Raster.Kernel.gaussian(deviation:radius:)` samples exp(−d²/2σ²) at whole distances out to the radius, by default three deviations rounded up; a deviation of zero is `identity`, and a negative or non-finite one fails with `.weight`. Its weights come from the scalar's own `exp`, so they are as repeatable across platforms as that function is.
- `convolved(horizontal:vertical:edge:mixing:)` replaces each pixel by the kernel-weighted average of its row neighbours, then does the same down the columns of the result. A weight at distance d from the kernel's middle multiplies the pixel d positions further along. A kernel of signed weights that do not sum to one is not offered: its result is a difference of pixels, not a pixel.
- Every average here, in sampling, resampling and convolution, is computed as a chain of interpolations: it starts from the first pixel with a non-zero weight and mixes in each next one at its weight divided by the weight so far. Two consequences hold for any interpolation that returns its first argument when both arguments are equal and stays between its arguments: a constant raster stays exactly constant, and no result leaves the range of the pixels averaged. With `identity` on both axes no interpolation is called and the raster is returned as it is.
- Convolving across and then down gives the same result as down and then across, up to rounding. With `.wrap`, a convolution keeps the mean of the pixels up to rounding.
- Away from the borders a convolution reads pixels directly, so its cost is one interpolation per kernel weight per pixel per axis. A full-HD raster of four `Float` channels under a 31-weight Gaussian on both axes took 0.45 s in a release build on one x86-64 core.
- `fragments(of:width:height:empty:_:)` samples triangles at pixel centres. A triangle is three `Point<3, Scalar>` of swift-point: x and y are positions on the raster and z is depth, which is what a projected mesh gives. For each pixel whose centre some triangle takes, your function is called once, in row order, with the nearest such triangle's position in the sequence, the centre's weights on that triangle's three corners in the order you listed them, and the depth those weights give; the pixel is what it returns, and its typed error is the operation's. A pixel whose centre no triangle takes is `empty`.
- A triangle is sampled at the centre and not averaged over the pixel's square. A depth and a set of weights belong to one point of one triangle; averaged over a square that several triangles share they belong to none, and which triangle is nearest cannot be decided between averages. A pixel is therefore taken or not, with nothing in between. For soft edges, sample at a multiple of the size and shrink with `resampled` and `.box`, mixing as your pixel mixes.
- A centre strictly inside a triangle is taken. A centre exactly on an edge is taken when the triangle lies to the right of that edge or, for an edge along a row, below it; a centre on a corner must be taken by both edges that meet there. This is the rule a pixel's own square follows, which holds its left and top border and not its right and bottom. Which way round the corners are listed makes no difference, and nothing is culled.
- Two triangles that share an edge, given by the same two points in either order, and lie on opposite sides of it never both take a centre and never both leave one that lies between them. Each edge is measured from the same end whichever triangle asks, so the two compute the same number with opposite signs, rounding included.
- Nearest means the smaller depth, as swift-projection maps the near plane to the lower depth bound. Of equal depths the triangle earlier in the sequence wins.
- A fragment's weights are not negative and add up to one, up to rounding; a corner that lies on a centre and is taken has weight exactly one. They are weights in the plane of the raster, and the depth is the corners' depths mixed by them, which is right for a depth that has been through a projection. A quantity that should follow perspective is corrected by the caller, with each corner's own divisor.
- A triangle without area, or with a coordinate that is not finite, takes nothing and still counts in the numbering. A triangle partly or wholly off the raster is clipped to it; no coordinate traps.
- `fragments(of:in:empty:_:)` does the same for the pixels of a `Raster.Region` and returns a raster of the region's size: exactly that region of the whole raster's result. The cost is three edge measures for every pixel centre in each triangle's bounding box, as far as the box lies in the region, then one pass over the pixels; it holds a triangle number and a depth for each pixel while it works.
- The `Geometry` trait adds `coverage(of:rule:width:height:tolerance:)`. Without the trait nothing of swift-geometry is built. The path is in raster space: one unit is one pixel, x runs along the columns and y along the rows, so y points down the picture, and a fill does not depend on which way a contour turns. The result holds, for each pixel, the part of its square that lies inside the path under the `Winding.Rule` of swift-direction, from 0 to 1, in whichever floating-point pixel type you ask for. It is computed in the path's scalar and converted at the end.
- The path is first made straight with `flattened(tolerance:)` of swift-geometry, and a tolerance that is not positive and finite throws `Geometry.Path.Error.tolerance`. Every subpath counts as closed by a straight line back to its start, whether or not it `isClosed`. A subpath with a corner that is not finite is left out.
- Of that polygon the coverage is the exact area, up to the rounding of the scalar's arithmetic. Nothing is sampled, and nothing is approximated where contours cross, touch or overlap inside a pixel, under either rule: each pixel row is cut into bands at every height where a side ends or two sides cross, inside a band the sides are walked from left to right with the winding number between them, and where the rule says inside, the area between two sides is shared out over the pixels it lies in. So the stroke outline of swift-geometry, whose pieces overlap with winding numbers above one, covers its region once under `nonzero`, and a pixel of which two overlapping pieces cover the same half is half covered, where adding the two pieces' coverages would call it full.
- The only approximation is the flattening. The coverage of a pixel differs from that of the true curve by no more than the area between curve and polygon inside it, and the coverage summed over the raster differs from the area of the path on the raster by no more than the tolerance times the length of its curves. A path of straight lines has no such error.
- A pixel that lies wholly inside the polygon is exactly 1 and a pixel wholly outside it is exactly 0, in the sense that no border of the filled region passes through the pixel's inside. No pixel is below 0 or above 1. A rectangle along the axes covers of each pixel the product of the lengths it overlaps along x and along y, exactly when those are exact in the scalar.
- Two paths that share only a border cover together what their union covers, up to rounding: added, their coverages leave no seam and no doubled edge. Whether a renderer adds coverages or lays one shape over another is its own choice and not made here.
- A path partly off the raster is clipped to it, a path wholly off it covers nothing, and a path around the whole raster covers every pixel exactly. No finite coordinate traps. A path with no subpaths, a subpath with no segments, and a contour that encloses no area cover nothing. A raster without pixels comes back empty.
- The coverage does not depend on the order of the subpaths, nor on the corner a closed contour starts from: the result is the same bit for bit, because the sides are put in an order of their own before anything is added.
- `coverage(of:rule:in:tolerance:)` covers only the pixels of a `Raster.Region` and returns a raster of the region's size. It equals that region of the whole raster's coverage up to rounding, and exactly where that is 0 or 1. `Raster.Region(enclosing:width:height:)` is the smallest region of whole pixels that holds a `Geometry.Rectangle` as far as it lies on a raster of that size, and has no pixels when the rectangle lies off the raster or has a bound that is not a number. The path's `boundingBox` is such a rectangle: outside the region that encloses it the coverage is zero.
- A fill sorts the sides once. Then each pixel row that the path reaches costs, for each of its bands, a pass over the sides that cross the row and a sort of the ones in the band; two sides that cross inside a row add a band. Last comes one pass over the columns between the path's leftmost and rightmost reach in the region. Rows and columns the path does not reach cost only the zeroes of the result, which is why a small shape is cheaper still in a region of its own.
- In a release build on one core of an Apple M3, into a full-HD raster of `Float` from a path of `Double` at a tolerance of 0.1: a disc of radius 500 (158 sides) took 2 to 3 ms; the stroke outline of a 400-corner spiral, 24 wide with round joins and caps (2021 sides, overlapping), 11 ms; a 3000-corner star whose spikes put some thousand sides across most rows, 100 ms; a triangle 40 pixels across, 0.2 ms on the whole raster and 0.02 ms in the region around it. Sampling 4080 triangles that tile the same raster took 63 ms.
